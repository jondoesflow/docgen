[10:16]

SM: Right. I want to walk the whole thing end to end. Somebody rings up and
wants some testing done. Take me from there to the money arriving.

DO: I'll start. Enquiries come in three ways. Direct to a named account

SM: What proportion each?

DO: Roughly - forty per cent direct to account managers, forty-five per cent

SM: What happens to a mailbox enquiry?

DO: One of my team picks it up, and they either quote it themselves if it's
standard, or they route it to the technical team if it needs an opinion.

SM: How do they decide?

DO: Experience, basically. There's a guidance document but it's from 2019 and
nobody looks at it. If it's a standard suite - say a contaminated land suite
on ten soil samples - they'll price it off the rate card. If it's got anything
unusual in it, or if it's over about five thousand pounds, it goes to
technical.

TB: And "technical" means one of four people, and if all four are busy it sits.

AF: How long does it sit?

TB: Days. That's your four-day quote turnaround right there.

SM: Where does the quote get built? What's the artefact?

DO: Excel. There are - Martin, how many quote templates are there?

MC: Twelve that I know of. There's probably more on people's local drives.

DO: Twelve templates, and the rate card is a separate spreadsheet that gets
updated centrally in January and then everyone saves a local copy and it drifts.

PR: This is one of the things that keeps me up at night, honestly. I don't have
confidence that the prices we're quoting are the prices we've approved.

SM: Is there a discount approval process?

PR: In theory. Account managers can go to ten per cent off list on their own
authority. Ten to twenty needs a sales manager. Above twenty needs me.

SM: And in practice?

PR: In practice it's an email to me that I approve on my phone, and there's no
record of it anywhere except my sent items. I found out in March that we'd been
running a thirty-two per cent discount for a customer for two years based on an
approval I gave in 2023 for a single job.

AF: Was that discovered by accident?

PR: Completely by accident. Our management accountant spotted it doing margin
analysis.

SM: OK. So quote goes out. Then what?

DO: Then usually nothing. We send it and we wait.

SM: No follow-up?

DO: Not systematically. The good account managers chase. Some don't. We have no
way of knowing which quotes are still live and which are dead.

PR: We think about forty per cent of quotes never get followed up at all. That
came out of a sales review last year.

SM: And when a customer does accept?

DO: They send us a purchase order, or sometimes just an email saying "go
ahead", and then samples turn up.

TB: Samples turn up is where my world starts. They arrive at goods-in, usually
by courier, sometimes hand-delivered by the site engineer, occasionally by
our own sampling team.

SM: Tell me about your own sampling team.

TB: Twenty-five field samplers. They go to site, take samples, fill in a
chain-of-custody form, bring them back or courier them in. That form is paper.
Carbon copy, three parts.

AF: Paper chain of custody in 2026?

TB: I know. But it's legally defensible and it works when there's no signal,
and half our sites have no signal. Any replacement has to work with no
connectivity, and it has to be signable by the client's site representative.

SM: Noted, that's a hard requirement. What happens when samples land?

TB: Goods-in books them in. Every sample gets a unique reference and a label.
Then we match them to the job - and that's a manual matching exercise, because
the sample references the customer used aren't the ones we use.

SM: How often does that go wrong?

TB: Weekly. We have a tray in Warrington we call the orphan tray. Samples that
have arrived with no paperwork we can match. Sometimes they sit for a fortnight
until someone rings us asking where their results are.

DO: And then it's my team who gets that call and has no idea what to tell them.

TB: Once they're booked in and matched, the job gets scheduled. Which means a
supervisor looks at a whiteboard and decides what goes on which instrument in
what order.

SM: A physical whiteboard?

TB: A physical whiteboard in Warrington, a spreadsheet in Bristol, and I
genuinely don't know what Aberdeen do. Aberdeen have their own way.

AF: What drives the scheduling decision?

TB: Turnaround promise first. Then instrument availability - some tests can only
run on certain kit and some kit is only in certain labs. Then batching, because
a lot of analyses are much cheaper per sample if you run twenty at once instead
of three. Then analyst availability and competency, because not everyone is
signed off on every method.

SM: Competency records - where do those live?

TB: A spreadsheet the quality team maintains. It's an accreditation
requirement, so it's actually well maintained, but it's not connected to
anything.

SM: Then the testing happens. Then what?

TB: Instrument produces raw data. Analyst reviews it, applies any calculations,
enters the result. Then it goes for technical check - a second qualified person
reviews and approves. That's a hard requirement, we can't release anything
without a second-person approval.

AF: Is that recorded in a way an auditor can follow?

TB: It's recorded, yes. Signature on a paper worksheet, or an initials field in
the lab system depending on the lab.

MC: Two of the four labs have a lab information system, two don't. Warrington
and Aberdeen do. Bristol and Dublin are on spreadsheets and paper.

AF: That's significant. We'll need to dig into that in the technical session.

TB: Then a certificate gets produced. Which is a Word template that someone
populates, checks, converts to PDF, and emails out.

SM: Manually?

TB: Manually. In Warrington there's a mail merge that does about seventy per
cent of it. Everywhere else it's typed.

DO: And then a copy gets filed on the shared drive, and a copy gets emailed to
whoever the customer contact was on the job, and that's the end of it as far as
the lab's concerned.

SM: And invoicing?

PR: Invoicing is a monthly batch. Finance pull a list of completed jobs and
raise invoices in the finance system. There's a reconciliation step where they
check the invoice value against the quoted value, and that's where things fall

SM: Changed how?

TB: Extra samples arrive. Or a result comes back and the customer says actually
can you also run this other suite on the same material. Or we've had to repeat
an analysis.

SM: Is a variation recorded anywhere?

TB: Sometimes there's an email. There's no formal variation process.

PR: We wrote off about a hundred and eighty thousand of unbillable work last
year. Some of that is genuine goodwill but a lot of it is work we did and
couldn't evidence that the customer had asked for.

AF: That's probably the single clearest financial case in what you've told us
so far.

PR: I agree, and the board agree.

SM: Turnaround options?

SM: What about approval - the discount thresholds Priya described. Do you want

PR: Yes. Enforced, with the approval recorded against the quote, and a record

SM: Should the routing be automatic based on rules, or manual?

TB: Rules would be better. Test type, value, whether it's a new customer.

SM: Can you write those rules down? Because "experience, basically" isn't

SM: Weighted pipeline by probability?

PR: Yes. Even a crude one would be better than what we have, which is nothing.

--- 5.2 JOB SETUP, SCHEDULING AND CAPACITY ---

SM: Tom, this is your area. What's the single thing that would make the biggest
difference?

TB: Knowing what's coming. If I could see, today, everything that's been quoted
and is likely to land in the next four weeks, converted into a rough estimate
of instrument hours and analyst hours by lab, I could actually plan.

AF: So capacity forecasting driven off the pipeline, not just off confirmed work.

TB: Exactly that. And I'd want to be able to say "we can't take that job in
Warrington in that window" before we commit to a date, not after.

SM: So a capacity check at the point of quoting.

TB: Ideally, yes. Even a warning would do. "This job would push Warrington's
ICP-MS over capacity in week 22."

SM: Is that a must or a want?

TB: [pause] I'd say the forecast view is a must. The automatic warning at quote
stage is a want. I can live with a human looking at a screen.

SM: Good, that's a useful distinction. Scheduling itself - do you want the
system to schedule, or do you want it to help a human schedule?

TB: Help a human. I've seen automatic scheduling in a lab and it was a disaster
because it doesn't know that the mass spec is temperamental on Mondays or that
Kevin is the only one who can get a decent result out of the older GC. Give me
a good view and let me drag things around.

AF: That's a much more achievable ask, and I'd support it. Fully automated
scheduling in an environment with that much tacit knowledge tends to get
overridden until people stop trusting it.

TB: Thank you. Someone finally.

SM: What about batching? You said batching drives cost.

TB: I'd like to see, for a given test method, all the samples awaiting that test
across all live jobs, with their due dates, so I can build an efficient batch
without missing a deadline. That's a manual eyeball job today and we get it
wrong.

SM: Across labs, or within a lab?

TB: Within a lab primarily. But there is a case for moving work between labs -
if Bristol is drowning and Aberdeen has capacity, we should be shipping samples.
We do that maybe once a month and it's always a phone call and a panic.

SM: So inter-lab transfer as a supported process?

TB: I'd like it. It's not a day one thing.

--- 5.3 SAMPLE TRACKING AND CHAIN OF CUSTODY ---

SM: The orphan tray. How do we kill it?

TB: Sample references need to be agreed before the samples arrive. If the job is
set up in advance with an expected sample list, and the labels are generated by
us and sent to the customer or the sampler before they take the sample, then
everything that arrives has our reference on it.

SM: So pre-registration of expected samples, and label generation.

TB: Yes. Barcode labels ideally. Then goods-in scans, and it books in against
the job automatically.

SM: What about samples that arrive that weren't expected?

TB: There has to be an exception path. You can't just reject them, they're
someone's site work and they might be irreplaceable. But it should be flagged
and someone should have to resolve it.

SM: Chain of custody. You said it must work offline and must be signable by a
third party on site.

TB: Correct. The sampler records what they took, where, when, in what container,
under what preservation. The client rep signs to confirm. Then every subsequent
handover - sampler to courier, courier to goods-in, goods-in to lab - gets
recorded.

AF: Is the full handover chain a legal requirement or a best practice?

TB: For most environmental work it's best practice that customers demand. For
anything that might end up in litigation - contaminated land disputes, pollution
incidents - it's effectively mandatory, and we've had our records subpoenaed
twice.

AF: Then we treat it as mandatory. What about sample condition and temperature?

TB: Yes, that's part of it. Some samples have to be kept below four degrees.
We currently record a temperature on arrival with a probe and write it on the
form. If a sample's out of spec we have to record that and flag it, because it
may invalidate the result.

SM: And holding times?

TB: Every method has a maximum holding time from sampling to analysis. If we
breach it, the result is qualified. I want the system to tell me when something
is approaching its holding time limit, because right now we find out afterwards.

SM: That's a strong requirement. Alerting on holding time expiry.

TB: It's probably the single most valuable thing on my list after capacity.

--- 5.4 RESULTS, APPROVAL AND CERTIFICATES ---

SM: The second-person approval. Non-negotiable?

TB: Completely non-negotiable, it's an accreditation requirement. And the
approver has to be someone who is signed off as competent on that method, which
means the system needs to know competency and enforce it.

SM: So competency records need to move out of the quality team's spreadsheet
and become live data.

TB: Yes, and the quality team will hate that and then love it.

SM: Can a result be changed after approval?

TB: It can be amended, but the original has to be retained, the amendment has to
be justified and re-approved, and any certificate we've already issued has to be
formally superseded with a new version. We can never delete anything.

AF: Full audit trail, no hard deletes, version control on issued documents.
Understood, and that's a fairly fundamental design constraint - I'm glad we've
got it this early.

SM: Certificates. What has to be on one?

TB: There's a defined content list from the accreditation standard - customer,
sample identification, method reference, result, uncertainty where applicable,
the accreditation mark against accredited tests only, deviations, and the
authorising signatory. It's prescriptive.

SM: Do certificate formats vary by customer?

DO: Yes, unfortunately. About fifteen of our larger customers have their own
format requirements, and three of them want us to submit results directly into
their own systems in a specific data format.

AF: That's an integration requirement, we'll pick it up in the technical
session. Roughly how many formats?

MC: Four different data formats that I know of. One of them is an industry
standard format for environmental data, the others are bespoke.

[ACTION: MC to collate examples of the four customer data export formats]

SM: What about interpretation? Do you compare results to thresholds?

TB: We do. For contaminated land we assess against screening values, and those
vary by land use - residential, commercial, allotment. The customer tells us
which to apply. We'd want that comparison automated with exceedances flagged,
because it's done by hand now and it's error-prone.

SM: Where do the screening values come from?

TB: Published sources, updated periodically, plus some customers have their own.
They'd need to be maintainable by our technical team without IT involvement.

MC: Please. Yes. Anything configurable by the business, not by me.

--- 5.5 CUSTOMER PORTAL AND SELF-SERVICE ---

SM: Denise, this is yours. If customers could do three things themselves, what
would you pick?

DO: One - see the live status of every job and every sample. Two - download

order without ringing us.

SM: In that priority order?

DO: Status first, absolutely. That's the sixty-four per cent.

SM: How many customers?

DO: About two thousand three hundred with activity in the last two years. But
it's very concentrated - our top fifty customers are seventy per cent of
revenue.

SM: So a portal that serves the top fifty well is most of the value.

DO: Yes, but the long tail is where most of the phone calls come from,
proportionally. A small customer with one job rings three times about it.

SM: Fair. Multiple users per customer?

DO: Definitely. On a big framework there might be twenty engineers who all want
access, and they should only see their own projects, not the whole account.

AF: So a permissions model within the customer organisation. Who administers
that - you or them?

DO: Ideally them. I do not want my team creating logins.

SM: What about notifications? Would a proactive email reduce the calls more than
a portal?

DO: [pause] That's a good question. Probably yes, actually. If every customer
got an email when their samples were booked in, and another when results were
issued, and a warning if the date was going to move, that might kill half the
calls without anyone logging into anything.

TB: The date moving one is the important one. Customers don't mind delays nearly
as much as they mind being surprised by delays.

PR: I'd rather do the notifications first and the portal second, if that's a
choice we get to make.

DO: I'd disagree slightly. Notifications don't help with the historical

they involve digging through the shared drive.

AF: You may not have to choose - they're often the same underlying work. But
it's useful to know that if we had to sequence them, there's a genuine
difference of view. Let's note that as an open decision rather than resolve it
here.

[PARKED: sequencing of proactive notifications vs full customer portal -
decision needed at design stage]

--- 5.6 REPORTING AND MANAGEMENT INFORMATION ---

SM: Priya, what can't you see today that you need to?

PR: Margin by job. I can see revenue by job and I can see cost at a lab level,
but I cannot tell you which jobs made money. I strongly suspect a meaningful

SM: What would you need to capture to get there?

PR: Analyst time, I suppose. Instrument time. Consumables. Subcontract costs.

TB: Analyst time is going to be contentious. My people are not going to fill in

AF: There's usually a middle ground - standard costs per test method, derived
from a periodic time study rather than continuous capture. You lose precision
but you gain something that's actually maintainable.

TB: That I could live with.

PR: That would be enough for me. I don't need it to be accounting-grade, I need
it to be good enough to make decisions with.

SM: What else?

PR: Conversion rates by account manager, by customer, by test type. Turnaround
performance by lab. Rework and repeat analysis rates - Tom, do we even know that
number?

TB: We know it per lab, roughly. It's between four and nine per cent depending
on the lab and the method, which is itself an interesting number.

PR: See, I'd never have known that. And subcontract spend, which I think is
around two point one million and I'm not confident in that figure.

SM: Who consumes these reports?
